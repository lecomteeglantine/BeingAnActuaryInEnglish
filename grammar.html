GRAMMAR FOR QUANTITATIVE ENGLISH

Grammar review

Not grammar for grammar’s sake: these are the structures you need to describe evidence, uncertainty, methods, change and recommendations precisely.

1 · Comparatives

Use: compare portfolios, periods and scenarios.

The motor portfolio is more volatile than the household portfolio. Claim frequency is lower, but severity is higher.

Avoid “more higher”. Use higher, significantly higher, slightly lower.

2 · Modals & hedging

Use: express uncertainty without sounding vague.

The increase may reflect claims inflation. The model could underestimate tail risk. This is likely to affect the reserve estimate.

Match certainty to evidence: may / might / could → likely to → strongly suggests.

3 · Passive voice

Use: focus on method or result when the actor is not important.

The model was calibrated on five years of data. Outliers were removed before validation.

Do not overuse it. “We tested three scenarios” is often clearer than a long passive sentence.

4 · Conditionals

Use: explain dependencies and scenarios.

If inflation remains high, claim severity will increase. If the retention were lower, the reinsurance premium would rise.

Use the first conditional for plausible consequences; the second for hypothetical alternatives.

5 · Trend language

Use: describe change over time accurately.

Claims rose by 12%. The loss ratio increased from 68% to 74%, a rise of six percentage points.

By = size of change. To = final value. Percent and percentage points are not interchangeable.

6 · Cause & consequence

Use: distinguish association from causal claims.

The increase coincided with higher repair costs. This may have contributed to the deterioration in the loss ratio.

Prefer cautious verbs when causality is not established: is associated with, may reflect, may contribute to.
